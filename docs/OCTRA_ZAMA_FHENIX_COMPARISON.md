# Octra compared with Zama and Fhenix

The most interesting difference is **where encrypted computation happens and what the application gets back**. In the Octra devnet trial, the program invoked encrypted operations through the Octra VM and stored serialized ciphertexts that the client retrieved and decrypted locally. In the Sepolia benchmark, the Zama and Fhenix contracts stored **handles**. Their on-chain calls registered operations, and off-chain services evaluated the ciphertexts and handled authorized decryption. [Octra trial](../logs/octra-lifecycle-once-2026-11-02-results/analysis.md) · [Zama coprocessor](https://github.com/zama-ai/fhevm/blob/main/docs/protocol/architecture/coprocessor.md) · [Fhenix Solidity implementation](https://github.com/FhenixProtocol/cofhe-contracts/blob/master/contracts/FHE.sol)

| Dimension | Octra devnet trial | Zama and Fhenix on Sepolia |
|---|---|---|
| **Programming model** | An AppliedML program calls encrypted VM primitives such as `fhe_add`. | Solidity contracts invoke FHE library functions such as `FHE.add` over typed handles. |
| **Result retrieval** | The client reads the persisted ciphertext and decrypts it with its local PVAC key. | The client reads the persisted handle and requests authorized decryption through the platform SDK. |
| **Arithmetic semantics** | The tested PVAC operations use field arithmetic. Division by a constant uses its modular inverse. | The benchmark uses 16-bit readings and 64-bit unsigned encrypted arithmetic, with integer division for `mean`. |
| **Cost accounting** | The trial records native fees in OU and VM effort as separate quantities. | Receipts record Ethereum gas. The contract transaction pays for on-chain work such as verification, permission updates, and operation dispatch. |

The **`mean` example** is the clearest difference for the paper. An even sum gave the expected answer on Octra, but that fixture concealed the semantic difference. For an odd sum, `(42 + 17) / 2` gives **29** with unsigned integer division. In the local Octra test, field division gave **85070591730234615865843651857942052893**. The tested Octra compiler also rejected `fhe_max` and rejected ordinary `max` on encrypted operands. That gives two distinct portability findings. An operation can be exposed with different semantics, or the compiler may lack an equivalent operation. [Octra arithmetic and compiler results](../logs/octra-lifecycle-once-2026-11-02-results/analysis.md)

The **cost of loading the key** is another concrete implementation difference. In the Octra pilot, inspection of the node code indicated that loading the registered public key consumed substantial VM effort before the arithmetic itself. In the later trial, addition confirmed with **2,560,073 units of effort**, just **73 units above** the preceding failed allowance. That makes the key-loading and effort-budget path worth discussing alongside the Solidity verification, ACL, and storage paths in Zama and Fhenix. [Octra feasibility notes](OCTRA_FEASIBILITY.md) · [Recorded addition attempts](../logs/octra-lifecycle-once-2026-11-02-results/analysis.md)

For the paper, Octra fits as a **workload portability case study**. The trial validated `notarize`, `add`, and scalar multiplication on small inputs, exposed the division mismatch, and identified the compiler limitation for `max`. Only `notarize` yielded a valid primary latency, so the comparison supports conclusions about **which parts of the workload transfer and how their execution paths differ**.
